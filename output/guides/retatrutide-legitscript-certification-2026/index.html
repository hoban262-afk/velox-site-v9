What Did CBS News' Investigation Actually Find?

CBS News published an investigation on 16 September 2026 reporting that more than 80 businesses advertising or selling retatrutide, Eli Lilly's investigational triple-hormone-receptor obesity drug, displayed certification badges from LegitScript, a private compliance company whose seal has become one of the main gatekeeping mechanisms for healthcare advertising online.[1] At least 35 of the identified companies featured their LegitScript certification badge on the exact same web page where they promoted retatrutide — a symbol of vetted legitimacy sitting directly beside marketing for a drug that has not received approval from any medicines regulator anywhere in the world.[1]

Selling retatrutide commercially to consumers is prohibited under US federal law while it remains an unapproved investigational drug, yet CBS found companies were still running ads for it on major platforms — something those platforms' own advertising policies are supposed to prevent for unlicensed prescription products.[1]

How Does LegitScript Certification Actually Work?

LegitScript is a private, for-profit monitoring and certification company, not a government regulator. Its certification has nonetheless become one of the only practical routes for a healthcare advertiser to get prescription-drug or telehealth ads approved on Facebook, Instagram, Google, YouTube and LinkedIn, because those platforms generally require LegitScript certification, or an equivalent, before allowing that category of advertising at all.[1] In effect, the badge functions as a trust signal for both platforms and prospective customers — it is meant to indicate that a business meets defined legal and marketing standards.

A certification badge answers a different question than “is this approved for human use?” CBS's reporting describes certified businesses that were, according to the investigation, advertising a drug prohibited from commercial sale — the badge did not stop that advertising from running, and in several cases appeared to lend it credibility.

This builds on an earlier CBS News investigation, published in June 2026, which identified more than 120 websites and dozens of clinics openly advertising or prescribing unapproved retatrutide — a pattern our earlier coverage summarised in the context of rising poison-centre exposure reports linked to black-market retatrutide. The LegitScript story adds a specific mechanism to that broader pattern: how some sellers were able to reach mainstream ad audiences at all.

How Does This Fit Eli Lilly's Broader Black-Market Crackdown?

The LegitScript findings land squarely within Eli Lilly's ongoing enforcement push against unauthorised retatrutide sellers. Lilly has said it has reported more than 200 entities engaged in illegal retatrutide sales to the FDA, the Department of Justice, state attorneys general and professional licensing boards, and it filed six federal lawsuits directly against sellers on 12 August 2026, which we covered in detail in our report on those lawsuits.[2]

Litigation targets specific sellers

Lilly's August lawsuits named six companies directly, several of which allegedly labelled retatrutide “research use only” while marketing it for human injection.[2]

The LegitScript story targets the ad pipeline

CBS's reporting instead examines how a much larger set of sellers — more than 80 — were able to reach consumers through mainstream advertising channels at all, regardless of how any individual seller labelled its product.[1]

Read together, the two stories describe the same underlying problem from different angles: a large, persistent black market for an unapproved drug, and the advertising and certification infrastructure that has, according to this reporting, sometimes made that market look more legitimate than it is.

Why a Certification Badge Is Not the Same as a Verified Research Reagent

It is worth being precise about what a certification badge like LegitScript's is actually designed to confirm. It signals to an advertising platform that a business has met defined licensing, marketing and compliance criteria — it is a statement about the seller's conduct, not a statement about the chemical identity or purity of any specific product a buyer receives. Nothing about a compliance certification tests what is in a vial.

That distinction matters directly for anyone sourcing a research compound. A genuine research reagent is not distinguished from a black-market product by a trust badge, a review score, or marketing language — it is distinguished by batch-specific identity and purity testing, documented in a certificate of analysis a buyer can actually inspect, combined with the complete absence of dosing instructions or human-use claims, which no lawful research-reagent listing should carry in the first place.

Velox Peptides supplies retatrutide strictly as an HPLC-verified in vitro research reagent, with a batch-specific certificate of analysis available on request rather than a marketing certification standing in for it — the opposite of the badge-as-trust-signal pattern CBS describes.

What Should UK Researchers Take From This?

CBS's investigation concerns US consumer advertising practices and a US-focused certification company; it has no bearing on the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 or MHRA guidance governing lawful research-reagent supply in Great Britain and Northern Ireland. But the underlying lesson generalises well beyond the US market: a supplier's marketing polish, review badges or certification logos are not a substitute for verifiable batch documentation when evaluating any peptide supplier, anywhere.

What did CBS News' LegitScript investigation find?
