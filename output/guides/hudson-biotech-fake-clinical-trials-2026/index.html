What Did the Investigation Into Hudson Biotech's Trials Find?

A 31 July 2026 investigation published on the Dr. Noc, PhD Substack identified a ClinicalTrials.gov registration, NCT07487363, for a study of TB-500 (a fragment of thymosin beta-4) in cardiovascular disease that opens with the sentence: “This fictional study is an example of a ClinicalTrials.gov-style record.”[1] That admission led the author to pull the sponsor's full filing history. The sponsor, listed as Hudson Biotech, had registered eight studies in total — all first submitted between February and March 2026, all listing the same study site (Peking University Shenzhen Hospital), and all still marked Recruiting at the time of publication.[1]

Three of the eight records disclose their own fabrication directly in the registry text: the TB-500 entry calls itself a “fictional study”; a Melanotan II entry opens with “This example interventional study record describes…”; and a tesamorelin entry carries “(Mock Study)” in its own title.[1] Two further entries are not new compounds at all — they reuse Eli Lilly's SURMOUNT-1 trial name and internal compound code (LY3437943) without authorisation. Lilly's own statement, as reported by the investigation, was unambiguous: “Lilly is aware of these registrations. We have no relationship with Hudson Biotech and did not authorize its use of our protocol identifiers or the SURMOUNT-1 name.”[1]

The investigation's conclusion was blunt about the batch as a whole: “Between the three self-labeled mock records and the two cloned Lilly trials, there is no question that the whole batch of 8 entries submitted by ‘Hudson Biotech’ is bogus,” adding that the BPC-157, MOTS-c, and GHK-Cu entries “read like ordinary trial records, with nothing indicating they are ‘fictional’ or ‘example.’”[1] That is a materially different situation from a single mislabelled record: it is a sponsor identity that, on the reporting available, does not check out.

Does This Affect the BPC-157 Trial Velox Covered Previously?

Yes, and directly. Our earlier guide to NCT07437547 reported Hudson Biotech's hamstring-strain study as BPC-157's first controlled human trial, based on the registration as it stood in August 2026. That record shares the same sponsor name, the same February–March 2026 filing window, and reporting places it inside the batch the investigation calls bogus.[1] The BPC-157 entry does not contain the explicit “fictional” or “mock” language that three sibling records do, so it cannot be confirmed fake on its own text alone — but its provenance is now, at minimum, unverified rather than credible.

Update to our earlier coverage. Treat NCT07437547 as an unconfirmed registration, not as evidence that a real hamstring-strain trial of BPC-157 is underway, until an independent source — the named hospital, a listed investigator, or a non-sponsor publication — confirms the study exists.

How Did a Fake Trial Get Onto an Official US Government Registry?

ClinicalTrials.gov, run by the National Library of Medicine, is a self-service registry. Sponsors submit their own study details, and the platform publishes them without independently verifying that a named hospital agreed to host the trial, that a listed investigator is real, or that the submitting company exists as described.[1] That design keeps registration fast for the overwhelming majority of legitimate trials, but it also means a fabricated entry can sit live for months. The investigation reports that at least one commercial drug-intelligence platform had already built a pipeline entry around the fake BPC-157 record, and that an AI chatbot had cited the fake TB-500 record back as real evidence — both signs of how quickly an unverified registration can be laundered into apparently credible secondary sources.[1]

How Should Researchers Verify a Clinical Trial Registration Before Citing It?

Confirm the site independently

Contact the named hospital's own clinical-trials or research office, or check whether the trial appears in that institution's own public trial listings, rather than relying on the registry page alone.

Check the sponsor's footprint

A legitimate sponsor company usually has activity outside a single registry entry — other trials with different sites and investigators, a verifiable corporate history, or coverage in trade press.

Look for a named investigator

A real trial typically has a principal investigator who is findable in the published literature or at the named institution. An entry with no locatable investigator is a warning sign.

Wait for a non-sponsor source

Treat topline results, recruitment claims, or safety data as unconfirmed until they are reported by a source other than the sponsor itself — a journal, a regulator, or independent trade press.

Frequently Asked Questions

What did the investigation into Hudson Biotech's clinical trials find?

A 31 July 2026 investigation by Dr. Noc, PhD found eight ClinicalTrials.gov registrations filed by a sponsor called Hudson Biotech, all first submitted between February and March 2026, all listing the same study site (Peking University Shenzhen Hospital) and all marked Recruiting. Three of the eight openly disclose their own fabrication in their record text, and two more reuse Eli Lilly's real SURMOUNT-1 trial name and internal compound code without authorisation.

Which peptide compounds are named in the fake trial batch?

Reported entries in the batch name BPC-157 (NCT07437547, an acute hamstring-strain study), TB-500 (NCT07487363, a cardiovascular-biomarker study that opens with the disclosure "This fictional study is an example of a ClinicalTrials.gov-style record"), Melanotan II, tesamorelin, MOTS-c, and GHK-Cu, alongside the two records cloned from Lilly's SURMOUNT-1 programme.

Does this affect the BPC-157 human trial Velox covered previously?

It should change how that registration is read. Velox's earlier guide to NCT07437547 treated it as BPC-157's first controlled human trial based on the registry listing alone. That listing shares the same sponsor, filing window, and site as entries the investigation says are fabricated, and the investigation's own account places the BPC-157 record inside the fraudulent batch. NCT07437547 does not carry a "fictional" or "mock" disclaimer itself, but no independent confirmation that the trial, its site, or its investigators exist has been found. Treat it as unverified rather than as evidence of a genuine ongoing study.

How did a fake trial get onto an official US government registry?

ClinicalTrials.gov is a self-service registry: the National Library of Medicine publishes what sponsors submit and does not independently verify that a named site, investigator, or sponsor company is real before a record goes live. That design, intended to keep registration fast and low-friction for legitimate trials, is also what let entries sit publicly for months, get ingested by commercial drug-intelligence databases, and get cited as real evidence before anyone flagged them.

How can researchers check whether a clinical trial registration is genuine?

Look for independent confirmation beyond the registry page itself: a named site that can be verified through the hospital's own trials office, a sponsor with a verifiable corporate history and other public activity, an investigator who is findable in the published literature, and results or press coverage from a source other than the sponsor. A record with no verifiable site, no sponsor footprint outside the registry, and language that reads like a template is a reason to treat the trial as unconfirmed rather than to cite it as evidence.
