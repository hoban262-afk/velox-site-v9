Former US Surgeon General Calls for a 'Middle Ground' on FDA Peptide Regulation

What Did Jerome Adams Argue in His 6 July 2026 STAT News Op-Ed?

On 6 July 2026, former US Surgeon General Jerome M. Adams, MD, MPH, published an opinion piece in STAT News titled "Regulating peptides: FDA should take a middle-ground approach."[1] Writing just over two weeks ahead of the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) hearing on 23–24 July, Adams argued against two extremes he sees dominating the debate over popular but unproven research peptides such as BPC-157 and TB-500: an outright ban that would push existing demand toward unregulated grey-market sources, and an unrestricted legalisation that would outpace the available safety evidence.

Instead, Adams called on the FDA to pursue what he described as "a structured, clinician-guided third way" — a framework designed to bring existing consumer demand under professional oversight, protect patients today, gather evidence for tomorrow, and prevent the worst outcomes of what he characterised as an unregulated boom in peptide use.[1]

Who Is Jerome Adams and Why Does His Position Carry Weight?

Adams served as the 20th Surgeon General of the United States from 2017 to 2021, appointed during the first Trump administration, and is now a faculty member at Indiana University School of Medicine.[2] He has become a regular contributor to health-policy commentary in outlets including STAT News, and has previously written critically about the current administration's health leadership, giving his intervention on peptide policy a degree of political independence from both the industry lobbying for looser rules and the FDA scientists who have flagged evidence gaps.

That positioning matters because the PCAC debate has, until now, largely played out between two camps already covered in our PCAC evidence-review guide: FDA scientific staff, whose 29 June 2026 briefing documents found no human trial data at all for TB-500 and KPV and insufficient evidence for BPC-157's proposed uses, and a newly reconstituted advisory panel that STAT News reported includes several members with commercial ties to the peptide industry. Adams's op-ed introduces a third, more centrist voice into that polarised exchange.

What Does a "Clinician-Guided" Middle Ground Actually Mean?

Not an outright ban

Adams argues that prohibiting compounds like BPC-157 and TB-500 outright would not eliminate demand — it would push existing users toward unregulated, untested grey-market sources with no quality control, arguably worsening the safety picture the FDA is trying to protect.

Not unrestricted access

Equally, Adams does not endorse the unrestricted legalisation favoured by some peptide advocates, arguing that consumer enthusiasm for these compounds has run well ahead of the controlled human trial data regulators say they need.

A structured, professionally overseen pathway

The proposed "third way" would bring peptide use under clinician oversight — framed as a mechanism to gather real-world safety data prospectively, rather than leaving evidence gaps to persist indefinitely under either prohibition or an unregulated market.

Adams's op-ed does not specify detailed regulatory mechanics for how a clinician-guided framework would be implemented within the FDA's existing 503A compounding structure, and the PCAC itself has no authority to create new categories beyond a Category 1 or Category 2 (or deferred) recommendation for each compound. The op-ed reads as a call for the philosophy underlying the PCAC's eventual decision, rather than a specific legislative or regulatory proposal.

How Does This Fit the Broader July 2026 PCAC Timeline?

The comment-forwarding window closed today. Written submissions to docket FDA-2025-N-6895 by 9 July 2026 are guaranteed to reach PCAC members before the hearing; later comments up to the docket's 22 July close are still accepted but not guaranteed the same review window. See our full hearing agenda guide for the day-by-day schedule.

Adams's op-ed lands at a specific point in a sequence already tracked on this site: twelve peptides, including all seven now under PCAC review, were removed from the FDA's 503A Category 2 restricted list in April 2026 (see our FDA Peptide Reclassification 2026 guide); FDA scientific staff then posted briefing documents on 29 June flagging thin or absent human evidence for several of the compounds; STAT News separately reported potential conflicts of interest among newly appointed PCAC panellists; and the docket's comment-forwarding deadline fell on 9 July 2026, the same day this article publishes. The PCAC hearing itself, and its Category 1 vote, follows on 23–24 July.

Whether or not the PCAC ultimately adopts anything resembling Adams's clinician-guided framework, his intervention illustrates that the debate has moved beyond a binary "ban versus legalise" framing in the days immediately preceding the vote.

How Does This Differ From Retatrutide's Regulatory Path?

It is worth distinguishing the PCAC compounding debate from the regulatory track followed by retatrutide, Eli Lilly's investigational triple-hormone-receptor agonist. Retatrutide is not before the PCAC and has no compounding-eligibility question attached to it; it is moving through the FDA's standard New Drug Application pathway on the strength of its own completed Phase 3 trials, with an NDA filing expected later in 2026 (see our retatrutide FDA approval timeline guide for the detailed sequence).

BPC-157, TB-500, and the other five PCAC compounds are not pursuing FDA drug approval at all in this process. The July hearing concerns only whether licensed US compounding pharmacies may prepare them under section 503A — a narrower, lower evidentiary bar than a full NDA, and one that, even if granted, does not make any of the seven an "FDA-approved" medicine in the way retatrutide would become if its NDA is eventually cleared.

What Should UK Researchers Take From This?

The PCAC process, the 503A compounding categories, and any clinician-guided framework the FDA might eventually adopt are US-specific mechanisms with no legal force in the UK. They do not change the regulatory status of BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, DSIP, or Epitalon as research reagents under UK law, and they have no bearing on the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 or MHRA guidance that governs supply in Great Britain and Northern Ireland.

What Adams's intervention does underline is that, even among senior US public-health figures, the evidentiary picture for these compounds is regarded as genuinely unsettled — not resolved in favour of either the "ban" or "legalise" camps. Frequently Asked Questions

What did Jerome Adams argue in his 6 July 2026 STAT News op-ed?

Former US Surgeon General Jerome Adams argued that the FDA should reject an outright ban on popular but unproven research peptides and instead pursue what he called a structured, clinician-guided third way, one that brings existing consumer demand under professional oversight, protects patients today, gathers evidence for tomorrow, and prevents the worst outcomes of an unregulated boom. He published the piece in STAT News ahead of the FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee's 23-24 July 2026 meeting.

Who is Jerome Adams?

Jerome M. Adams, MD, MPH, served as the 20th Surgeon General of the United States from 2017 to 2021 under the first Trump administration. He is now a faculty member at Indiana University School of Medicine and a regular contributor to health-policy commentary in outlets including STAT News.

What is the FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee reviewing in July 2026?

The PCAC meets 23-24 July 2026 to evaluate seven peptides for the 503A Bulk Drug Substances Category 1 list: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500 and MOTS-c on 23 July, and DSIP (emideltide), Semax and Epitalon on 24 July. A Category 1 recommendation would formally permit licensed US compounding pharmacies to prepare these substances under section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act.

How does the debate over compounding peptides differ from retatrutide's regulatory path?

They are entirely separate tracks. Retatrutide is an investigational drug moving through Eli Lilly's standard New Drug Application pathway, following completed Phase 3 trials, toward a possible FDA approval as a prescription medicine. BPC-157, TB-500 and the other PCAC compounds are not filing for drug approval; the July hearing concerns only whether licensed US compounding pharmacies may prepare them under section 503A, a narrower compounding-eligibility question that does not equate to FDA approval.

Does this US policy debate affect UK research-peptide supply?

No. The PCAC process and any resulting FDA compounding decision apply only to US-licensed compounding pharmacies. UK researchers purchasing BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax, KPV, DSIP or Epitalon as research reagents operate under UK law and MHRA guidance, which are independent of US 503A classifications regardless of how the FDA ultimately rules.
