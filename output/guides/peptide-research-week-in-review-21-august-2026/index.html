What Happened in Peptide Research This Week (14-20 Aug 2026)?

This week split cleanly into two tracks running in parallel. At the commercial top of the GLP-1 market, Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk escalated their advertising fight into a live court motion, with Novo asking a federal judge to immediately halt Lilly's Zepbound and Mounjaro campaigns.[1] Underneath that fight, the underlying science kept moving in smaller, more technical steps: a preclinical retatrutide study complicated the assumption that weight loss automatically fixes liver disease,[2] a Yale mechanistic study reframed how semaglutide actually engages the brain's hunger circuitry,[4] and BPC-157 — a compound with decades of animal data and almost no human evidence — reached active recruitment in its first-ever controlled human trial.[6]

None of these four stories is a single dramatic headline on its own. Read together, they show a market where the commercial stakes are rising faster than the evidence base underneath it — which is exactly the gap researchers, not marketers, are paid to close.

Why Is Novo Nordisk Trying to Block Eli Lilly's Advertising?

Federal litigation — motion filed 14 August, considered 17 August 2026
Novo Nordisk seeks a preliminary injunction against Lilly's Zepbound and Mounjaro ads

Novo Nordisk filed a motion for a preliminary injunction in its Lanham Act lawsuit against Eli Lilly, asking the U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey to immediately stop Lilly running ad campaigns Novo calls deceptive. Novo's core complaint is unchanged from its original July filing: the ads compare Lilly's highest approved Zepbound and Mounjaro doses against older, lower-dose Wegovy and Ozempic data, without disclosing Novo's newer, higher-dose formulations approved in March 2026. Novo says the Zepbound campaign referencing the SURMOUNT-5 head-to-head trial has generated roughly 700 million impressions since April 2026.[1]

Full breakdown: Why Did Novo Nordisk Seek an Injunction Against Lilly's GLP-1 Ads?

The court considered the motion on 17 August 2026; no ruling had been publicly reported as of 20 August. Lilly maintains its advertising is accurate and grounded in "the most direct scientific evidence available" — namely SURMOUNT-5, a genuine head-to-head randomised trial comparing Zepbound to Wegovy. Novo Nordisk's chief executive publicly defended the litigation on 12 August, saying "competition has to be fair."[3] Whichever way the injunction motion goes, it will not resolve the underlying false-advertising claim, which continues toward trial regardless.

What Did the New Retatrutide MASH Study Find?

Preclinical study — Obesity, 2026
Retatrutide cuts weight and liver fat in MASH mice and hamsters, but not fibrosis scores

Briand et al. dosed diet-induced obese MASH mice and hamsters with retatrutide for five weeks. Body weight fell about 31% relative to vehicle (p<0.0001), and hepatic triglycerides fell roughly 50% in both species — a substantial metabolic effect. But histopathological fibrosis and inflammation scores, read by a blinded pathologist, did not improve significantly over the same period.[2]

Full breakdown: Retatrutide MASH Mouse & Hamster Study: Liver Fat vs Fibrosis

The result is a useful corrective to a common shorthand: that shrinking liver fat on imaging automatically means the liver disease itself is resolving. A separate, human Phase 2a retatrutide trial in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease reported an 86% reduction in liver fat on MRI in 2024,[2] but fibrosis is a structural, histological endpoint that does not always move in lockstep with fat content or body weight — in rodents or, the data increasingly suggests, in humans. This is preclinical rodent data on an investigational compound and does not represent clinical outcomes.

What Does the AgRP Neuron Study Mean for How Semaglutide Works?

Mechanistic study — PNAS, published 4 August 2026
Yale researchers find semaglutide recruits, rather than silences, hunger neurons

d'Avila, Horvath and colleagues at Yale School of Medicine found that sustained semaglutide treatment activates and recruits hypothalamic AgRP hunger neurons in female mice, rather than suppressing this circuitry as the field had generally assumed. When the researchers silenced or ablated AgRP neurons, semaglutide's full weight-lowering effect was blunted even though food intake was still reduced — implicating a second, glucocorticoid-linked pathway working alongside appetite suppression.[4]

Full breakdown: New PNAS Study: AgRP Neurons and GLP-1 Weight Loss

Yale's own summary called it a finding that "may change how we think about GLP-1s," because it suggests the drug class's effect is not a single simple mechanism but at least two interacting circuits.[5] The data is mouse-only and female-only, and it concerns semaglutide, a licensed medicine and not a compound Velox Peptides supplies — but the mechanistic principle is directly relevant to how any GLP-1-class or multi-agonist molecule, including retatrutide, might be studied going forward.

What's Happening With BPC-157's First Human Trial?

Clinical trial — NCT07437547, recruiting since February 2026
Hudson Biotech's randomised, placebo-controlled BPC-157 trial reaches active recruitment

Hudson Biotech is running a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of BPC-157 in 120 adults with MRI-confirmed acute grade II hamstring strain, dosing for 14 days with co-primary endpoints of return-to-sport time and Day-14 MRI injury volume. It has been recruiting since February 2026, with primary completion estimated for February 2027.[6]

Full breakdown: BPC-157 Gets Its First Controlled Human Trial

This matters because BPC-157 has one of the widest gaps between animal data and human evidence of any compound in this catalogue: decades of rodent healing studies, but no completed, properly controlled human RCT until now. A 2015 registration (NCT02637284) never posted results.[6] The trial also lands a few weeks after the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted narrowly to recommend BPC-157 for compounding despite career scientists warning the existing evidence base was too thin to establish safety or effectiveness — making an actual controlled human dataset, whenever it reads out, unusually consequential.

How Do This Week's Stories Connect?

Commercial stakes and research evidence are moving on different clocks. A rivalry between two approved-drug manufacturers escalated into a court motion over how many millions of consumers see which ad, while the actual science underneath — what retatrutide does to a fibrotic liver, what neurons semaglutide really engages, whether BPC-157 works in a human body at all — is still being established one incremental study at a time.

None of this week's four stories changes what any compound is approved for or proven to do. A court considering an injunction motion is not a verdict on either company's advertising claims. A rodent liver-fat result is not a human fibrosis outcome. A mouse neuron-circuit finding is not a new mechanism confirmed in people. And a trial reaching recruitment is not a trial reporting results. For researchers, the throughline is the same one this roundup returns to most weeks: read the primary data, not the headline built on top of it.
