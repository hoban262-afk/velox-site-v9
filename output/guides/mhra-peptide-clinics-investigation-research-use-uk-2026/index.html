What Is the MHRA Investigating at UK Peptide Clinics?

In April 2026, trade and national press reported that the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) was investigating UK clinics that advertise peptide injections, following a Guardian investigation into clinics offering experimental peptides and describing their benefits on their websites.[1][2] The question under review, as reported, is whether those clinics are breaking the law by making medicinal claims.[3]

The reported regulatory position is short. A clinic that makes medicinal claims for a product is treated as selling a medicine, and a medicine needs a UK marketing authorisation under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 unless an exemption applies. The MHRA was also reported as saying there is little scientific evidence behind many of the health and wellness claims being made for these products.[1][2]

Source caveat. We could not retrieve the original Guardian article or an MHRA press release when preparing this guide. The facts above come from the trade-press reports listed in the references. Check the MHRA's own publications before relying on any specific wording.

Which Claims and Compounds Were Reported?

Coverage described clinics promoting peptide injections under headings such as anti-ageing, weight management, injury recovery and metabolism. BPC-157 and MOTS-c were named among the products involved.[2][3] The reporting concerns the marketing language, not laboratory studies on those molecules.

That distinction matters for researchers. Both compounds have a preclinical literature, and the evidence base is a live debate. Our BPC-157 literature dispute guide covers how concentrated that literature is among a few author groups, and our MOTS-c mitochondrial bioenergetics guide covers the preclinical work. Neither is a licensed UK medicine. Published preclinical findings do not establish that either product is safe or effective in people, which is the gap regulators are pointing at.

How Does This Differ From the MHRA Raids on Illegal Retatrutide Manufacturing?

The clinic investigation and the MHRA's criminal enforcement raids are separate workstreams. The raids targeted illegal premises making counterfeit injection pens, including products labelled as retatrutide and tirzepatide; one operation in Lincolnshire in February 2026 was reported to have seized nearly 2,000 doses.[4] Our guide to the MHRA retatrutide manufacturing raids sets out those cases with gov.uk sources.

The clinic probe is about advertising and claims. The raids are about illegal manufacture and supply of finished consumer products. Both are enforced under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, and both sit alongside the June 2026 joint MHRA, ASA and GPhC warning on pipeline-drug advertising, covered in our UK GLP-1 advertising crackdown guide.

What Does It Mean for Research-Use Supply?

On the reporting reviewed, the enforcement concern is conduct: medicinal claims and consumer-facing promotion. Nothing in it addresses compounds supplied as in vitro research reagents to qualified researchers with batch documentation and no health claims. Our guide on the legal status of research peptides in the UK sets out that boundary in full. This is a summary of third-party news, not legal advice, and the MHRA has not, in the sources we reviewed, issued guidance specific to research-reagent suppliers.

For laboratories, the practical points are documentary rather than promotional. Check that a supplier publishes a Certificate of Analysis per batch, that product pages carry research-use-only statements, and that no page implies use in people. Retatrutide, for example, remains an investigational pharmaceutical candidate in Eli Lilly's licensed development programme; the research-grade retatrutide reference material is a separate, laboratory-only product with no medicinal status.

What Should Researchers Watch Next?

As of 20 October 2026 we found no published outcome from the April investigation: no named clinics, enforcement notices or sanctions. Possible next signals are an MHRA statement on its findings, ASA rulings on clinic advertising, and any guidance addressing how the claims rules apply to peptide products generally. We will update this guide if the MHRA publishes any of these.

Frequently Asked Questions

What did the MHRA announce about UK peptide clinics in April 2026?

Following a Guardian investigation, the MHRA said it was investigating whether UK clinics advertising peptide injections were breaking the law by making medicinal claims. Trade press reported the regulator's position that clinics cannot make medicinal claims about peptides, and that products promoted with such claims are treated as medicines.

Which peptides were mentioned in the coverage?

Trade reporting named BPC-157 and MOTS-c, among others, as examples of products promoted by clinics. None of these is licensed as a medicine in the UK, and the reported concern was the claims made for them, not laboratory research into them.

Does the investigation change the legality of research-use peptides?

Nothing in the reporting we reviewed changes the legal status of compounds supplied as in vitro research reagents. The reported issue is conduct: medicinal claims and consumer-facing promotion. Supply with no such claims, for laboratory research only, is a different category. This is not legal advice.

Is this connected to the MHRA raids on illegal retatrutide manufacturing?

They are separate matters. The raids concerned counterfeit finished injection pens made at illegal premises and seized by the MHRA's Criminal Enforcement Unit. The clinic investigation concerns advertising and claims by clinics. Both sit under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012.

Has the MHRA published an outcome?

Our scan on 20 October 2026 found no published outcome, enforcement action or sanction arising from the April clinic investigation. We will update this guide if the MHRA publishes one.
